Exam mode 📝

For a learner who already knows the technique and wants a compact answer.

1. Set J = adj(A) and find its determinant.
2. For a 3 × 3 matrix, use the identity.
3. Find the adjoint of J.

Final answer

Detailed mode 🧩

Let's unpack every important connection, one small step at a time.

1What are we actually given?

We do not know the original matrix A. We know its adjoint — another matrix made by finding cofactors and transposing them. Call the given matrix J.

Think: J is a clue about A, not A itself.
2Find the determinant of J.

Expand along the first row. Cover the row and column of each chosen number to make a 2 × 2 determinant.

The smaller determinants are 24, −20 and 24. Take care with the alternating +, −, + signs.

3Why does ±4 appear?

For any 3 × 3 matrix, the determinant of its adjoint equals the square of its determinant.

Both 4 × 4 and (−4) × (−4) equal 16.

4Find the adjoint of J.

First calculate each cofactor of J (the signed 2 × 2 determinants). Arrange them into the cofactor matrix:

Transpose it: rows become columns. This is adj(J).

5Recover A using the second identity.

For a 3 × 3 matrix, taking the adjoint twice gives its determinant multiplied by the original matrix.

Divide every entry by +4 or −4, not just one entry.

We found A!
6Does the answer really work?

The positive matrix has determinant +4; its negative has determinant −4. Both have the given adjoint. In a 3 × 3 matrix, cofactors use 2 × 2 products, so changing the sign of every entry leaves its adjoint unchanged.

✅ Always remember: ± applies to the whole matrix. You cannot choose signs separately for the nine entries.
